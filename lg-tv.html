The TV Was Talking Behind My Back

A hardware channel said 216 million smart TVs were watching their owners. One of those TVs was in my living room. So I put it behind a Kali box and listened to what it actually said.

SS
Sruthi S
Security Analyst  ·  Published September 2026  ·  12 min read

It started with a video. Gamers Nexus put out a long investigation, made with Level1Techs and a few independent researchers, arguing that a huge number of LG smart TVs quietly recognise what is on screen, map the other devices on your home network, and feed all of it into an advertising pipeline. Ars Technica picked it up. LG pushed back on the strongest parts. The usual shape of a modern privacy story.

Here is the part that got me. I watched the whole thing on my own LG TV. There is something absurd about learning your television might be profiling you while it sits three feet away, glowing patiently, waiting for you to finish the documentary about itself. I did not want to take anyone's word for it, neither the channel's nor the manufacturer's. I wanted to see the packets with my own eyes.

So this is not a summary of someone else's research. This is a lab notebook. I own the TV, I own the network I tested it on, and everything below is either something I captured myself or something I am openly flagging as beyond what my setup could prove. That line matters more than any single finding, so I keep drawing it.

01 Building the wiretap

The problem with a smart TV is that there is nothing to detonate. My usual routine is to drop a sample in an isolated VM and watch it misbehave. A television has no sample. It has behaviour, and the only way to see behaviour is to sit in the middle of its conversations.

My TV is wireless only, no Ethernet port, so I could not just splice a cable and sniff it. Instead I turned my Kali laptop into the network the TV lives on. The laptop took its internet in over an Ethernet cable to my router, which freed its Wi-Fi radio to broadcast a small isolated hotspot. The TV joined that hotspot. From that point on, every packet the TV sent had to pass through my laptop, where I could record it. A switchboard, and I was the operator.

# laptop becomes the network the TV joins $ sudo nmcli device wifi hotspot ifname wlan0 ssid TVLAB password '********' Device 'wlan0' successfully activated # find the TV once it connects $ ip neigh show dev wlan0 10.42.0.74 lladdr e4:75:dc:4b:52:73 REACHABLE

That hardware address is its own small confirmation. The prefix e4:75:dc is registered to LG Electronics, so the network itself agreed I was watching the right box and not some stray phone that had wandered onto the hotspot.

I recorded with tcpdump and read the results with tshark. I never decrypted anything. Everything here comes from two places that stay readable even on encrypted traffic: the domain names the TV looked up, and the destination server name written in the clear at the start of every TLS handshake. That tells you who the TV talks to. It does not tell you what is inside the envelope, and I am careful about that all the way down.

02 What an idle TV says when it thinks nobody is listening

The first capture was the honest one. Factory settings, straight out of setup, TV sitting on its home screen. I did not open an app. I did not play anything. I just let it breathe for a few minutes and watched.

Thirteen hundred packets from a television doing nothing. And when I sorted the destinations by how often it reached each one, the busiest name in the whole capture was not LG, and not Netflix. It was a content recognition company.

TLS SNI · FACTORY SETTINGS · IDLE HOME SCREEN · ~3 MIN
tkacr731.alphonso.tv×8
mercury.alphonso.tv×1
us.rdx2.lgtvsdp.com (LG platform)×1

Alphonso is an automatic content recognition business. Its whole reason to exist is identifying what is on a screen and turning that into an advertising profile. And look at the first hostname. There is no reading between lines here. The letters acr are sitting right in the middle of it. This is LG's Live Plus feature, the content recognition layer, reaching out on repeat while I did absolutely nothing.

I ran it again later under controlled conditions, ten minutes this time, Live Plus confirmed on, still idle. Same story. The acr hosts came back, joined by a second one and an Alphonso provisioning host. Two separate captures, on my own hardware, both catching the fingerprinting layer awake at idle. That is the finding I am most confident in, and it is the one I would stake the whole post on.

03 The ad machine

When the TV was actually being used, with the home screen alive and content moving, the picture widened into something you could almost hear humming. The content recognition hosts stepped back and a full advertising supply chain stepped forward. LG's own ad server led it, followed by a roll call of exchanges and platforms you would recognise from any discussion of programmatic advertising on the open web.

TLS SNI · ACTIVE USE · TOP DESTINATIONS
us.ad.lgsmartad.com (LG's own ad server)×23
na-prog-pix.adstk.io (ad exchange)×13
na-imp-pix.adstk.io×13
vid-io-iad.springserve.com (video ads)×11
events.tremorhub.com (ad platform)×11
*.amazon-adsystem.com (Amazon)×22
cdn-aas-campaigns.alphonso.tv (Alphonso, ad side)×10
static.doubleclick.net (Google)×1

Notice the small detail inside Alphonso. The idle capture reached the content recognition hosts. This one reached a campaign delivery host instead. Same company, two different jobs, two different subdomains. A television that both watches what is on screen and serves the advertising that decision feeds. Worth writing down precisely, because sloppiness here is how a real finding gets dismissed as hype.

04 The question I could not answer cleanly

This is the part where most write-ups reach for a headline they have not earned, so I want to be plain. The obvious experiment is simple to say and hard to do. Turn the privacy settings off, capture again under identical conditions, and see whether Alphonso disappears. If it vanishes, the opt-out works. If it survives, that is the story with teeth.

I could not get a clean version of that comparison, and the reason is itself instructive. My idle capture with the settings on was calm and steady, about a thousand packets. Every time I disabled Live Plus and tried to match it, the TV refused to cooperate. Twice it drifted into a near sleep and produced almost no traffic, which makes an empty Alphonso result meaningless, because a silent TV and a TV that stopped phoning home look identical in the data. Once it swung the other way into a busy, ad heavy state with fifty thousand packets, and the content recognition hosts were absent there too, exactly as they are absent in every busy capture regardless of the setting.

So the on screen state, idle versus active, moves the traffic far more than the toggle does. Until I can hold that state perfectly still across both runs, I cannot honestly credit the opt-out for anything. The one narrow thing I can say, from a capture taken with the settings off, is that disabling Live Plus did not silence the wider advertising contact. The ad servers kept answering. Whether that is a failure of the opt-out or simply the difference between limiting targeting and stopping contact is a question I am still chasing.

The cleanest finding I have is not an accusation. It is a fact: at idle, on factory settings, this television's single most frequent contact was a content recognition company. Everything past that, I am still measuring.

05 What I will not claim

A DNS and SNI level view has hard edges, and pretending otherwise would poison everything above.

Contents of the traffic

I saw that the TV talked to Alphonso. I did not open the envelopes. I cannot tell you what, if anything, was fingerprinted, only that the machinery was active and busy.

The audio recording claim

The most alarming part of the original investigation, the idea of a set capturing audio, is simply outside what a network capture can confirm. LG disputes it directly and says its TVs do not continuously record or transmit conversations. I neither verified nor refuted it. I am pointing you at the people who raised it and staying in my lane.

Network device scanning

The investigation described TVs mapping other gadgets on the home network. I did not test this. The multicast chatter that showed up in my captures came from other devices in my house announcing themselves, two laptops and a smart bridge, not from the TV enumerating anything. Absence of proof, not proof of absence.

The vulnerabilities

The disclosed research includes remote code execution findings still moving through responsible disclosure. I deliberately did not go near them. This was an observation exercise, not an exploitation one, and there is a bright line between the two that I am not interested in crossing.

06 Where LG stands

Fairness demands the manufacturer's position sit right next to the findings. LG's stated line, given to the press covering this story, is that features like content recognition, voice, and interest based advertising are opt-in. The company says that if you do not consent to the relevant agreement, your content recognition data is not used for advertising, and that you can review or revoke these permissions in settings at any time. It also says its televisions do not continuously record or transmit conversations.

That claim is testable, at least the advertising half of it, and testing it is exactly the controlled experiment I owe this post a second attempt at. Read section four as an open thread, not a closed case.

07 Lock down your own set

Whatever the opt-out does or does not stop at the network level, turning it off is still the right move, and it costs you nothing. Menu names drift between models and firmware, so hunt around if these do not match yours exactly.

  1. Turn off Live Plus. On recent sets it lives under All Settings, then Support, then Privacy and Terms. On older ones try General, System, Additional Settings.
  2. Under User Agreements, opt out of Viewing Information, Voice Information, and Interest Based Advertising. On 2022 and newer sets there is an oddly named "Who Where What" to uncheck too.
  3. Switch on Do Not Sell My Personal Information on that same privacy screen.
  4. Check these again after every major firmware update. They have a habit of quietly switching themselves back on.
  5. Put the TV on a separate guest or IoT network so it cannot see the rest of your devices, whatever it does or does not do with that visibility.
NOTE TO SELF, AND TO YOU The value of doing this yourself is not that you will out-investigate a team of researchers with better gear. It is that you stop having to choose which press release to believe. The packets do not have a marketing department. Set up the wiretap on your own hardware, watch for ten quiet minutes, and the argument turns into a measurement.

I am going back for the controlled opt-out capture once I have tamed the TV's sleep timer. When I have a matched pair I trust, I will update this post with the number, whatever it turns out to be. That is the deal with field notes. They are honest about the parts that are still running.
